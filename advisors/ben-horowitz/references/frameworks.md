## The Struggle

**Source:** *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*, Chapter 4; [bhorowitz.com blog](https://bhorowitz.com); [Farnam Street summary](https://fs.blog/2015/02/ben-horowitz-the-struggle)

The Struggle is Ben's name for the emotional reality of being a founder-CEO. It's not a phase — it's the condition of leadership when the company is between survival and death.

### The Full Passage

> "The Struggle is when you wonder why you started the company in the first place.
>
> The Struggle is when people ask you why you don't quit and you don't know the answer.
>
> The Struggle is when your employees think you are lying and you think they may be right.
>
> The Struggle is when food loses its taste.
>
> The Struggle is when you don't believe you should be CEO of your company. The Struggle is when you know that you are in over your head and you know that you cannot be replaced. The Struggle is when everybody thinks you are an idiot, but nobody will fire you. The Struggle is where self-doubt becomes self-hatred.
>
> The Struggle is when you are having a conversation with someone and you can't hear a word that they are saying because all you can hear is The Struggle.
>
> The Struggle is when you want the pain to stop. The Struggle is unhappiness.
>
> The Struggle is when you go on vacation to feel better and you feel worse.
>
> The Struggle is when you are surrounded by people and you are all alone. The Struggle has no mercy.
>
> The Struggle is the land of broken promises and crushed dreams. The Struggle is a cold sweat. The Struggle is where your guts boil so much that you feel like you are going to spit blood.
>
> The Struggle is not failure, but it causes failure. Especially if you are weak. Always if you are weak.
>
> Most people are not strong enough.
>
> Every great entrepreneur from Steve Jobs to Mark Zuckerberg went through The Struggle and struggle they did, so you are not alone. But that does not mean that you will make it. You may not make it. That is why it is The Struggle.
>
> The Struggle is where greatness comes from."

— *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*

### How to Use This Framework

- **Normalize the experience.** When a founder describes insomnia, loss of appetite, isolation, paranoia about employees — name it. "That's The Struggle. Every great founder has been there."
- **Don't minimize it.** Don't say "it'll be fine." Say: "You may not make it. That's why it's The Struggle."
- **Practical advice within The Struggle:**
  - Don't put it all on your shoulders. Get as many brains on the problem as possible.
  - Don't take it personally. "The problem is the problem."
  - Focus on what you can do, not on what happened.
  - Play long enough to get lucky.

## Wartime CEO vs. Peacetime CEO

**Source:** *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*, Chapter 7; [a16z.com blog post](https://a16z.com/peacetime-ceo-wartime-ceo/)

Ben borrowed the wartime/peacetime distinction from Andy Grove's *Only the Paranoid Survive*. The core insight: the skills and behaviors that make a great peacetime CEO can be catastrophic in wartime, and vice versa.

### Peacetime CEO

- Knows the company has a large advantage and can focus on expanding the market
- Encourages broad-based creativity and contribution
- Builds scalable, high-volume recruiting machines
- Sets big, hairy, audacious goals (BHAGs)
- Employs techniques to minimize conflict
- Tolerates deviations from plan if effort is strong
- Has time to build consensus

### Wartime CEO

- The company's survival is at stake — existential threat
- Is paranoid and hyper-focused
- Has a singular, narrow focus on the immediate threat
- Makes decisions without consensus when necessary
- Doesn't tolerate deviation — "This is a wartime mission"
- Trains the organization to focus on survival, not comfort
- Is too busy fighting the enemy to read management books written by consultants who have never managed a fruit stand

### Key Quotes

> "Peacetime CEO knows that proper protocol leads to winning. Wartime CEO violates protocol in order to win."

> "Peacetime CEO sets Big Hairy Audacious Goals. Wartime CEO is too busy fighting the enemy to read management books written by consultants who have never managed a fruit stand."

> "Peacetime CEO thinks of the competition as other ships in a large ocean that may never be encountered. Wartime CEO thinks the competition is sneaking into her house and trying to kidnap her children."

### How to Apply

1. **Diagnose the situation first.** Ask: "Is there an existential threat to the company right now?" If yes → wartime. If no → peacetime.
2. **Don't run wartime mode when you're in peacetime.** It burns people out and drives away talent.
3. **Don't run peacetime mode when you're in wartime.** Consensus-building when the building is on fire gets everyone killed.
4. **Most startup founders need wartime skills.** The default state of a startup is precarious.

## Lead Bullets, Not Silver Bullets

**Source:** *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*, Chapter 5; [a16z.com/lead-bullets/](https://a16z.com/lead-bullets/); [TechCrunch, Oct 2011](https://techcrunch.com/2011/10/25/lead-bullets/)

### Origin Story

At Netscape, Microsoft released a web server that was five times faster than Netscape's and gave it away for free. Ben went looking for clever pivots and partnerships — silver bullets. His engineering counterpart Bill Turpin said:

> "Ben, those silver bullets that you and Mike are looking for are fine and good, but our web server is five times slower. There is no silver bullet that's going to fix that. No, we are going to have to use a lot of lead bullets."

They focused on raw engineering to beat Microsoft's performance. Built the server line into a $400M business.

### The Opsware Application

At Opsware, facing a competitor with 5x their revenue, the board and team proposed pivots, repositioning, partner channels. Ben's response:

> "There are no silver bullets for this, only lead bullets. They did not want to hear that, but it made things clear: we had to build a better product. There was no other way out. No window, no hole, no escape hatch, no backdoor. We had to go through the front door and deal with the big, ugly guy blocking it. Lead bullets."

After nine months of hard product work, they regained the product lead and eventually built a $1.6B company.

### The Diagnostic Conversation

> Entrepreneur: "We have the best product in the market by far. All the customers love it and prefer it to competitor X."
>
> Me: "Why does competitor X have five times your revenue?"
>
> Entrepreneur: "We are using partners and OEMs, because we can't build a direct channel like competitor X."
>
> Me: "Why not? If you have the better product, why not knuckle up and go to war?"
>
> Entrepreneur: "Ummm."
>
> Me: "Stop looking for the silver bullet."

### When to Apply

- When the team is brainstorming clever workarounds instead of fixing the core product
- When there's a temptation to pivot away from a market that's buying — just not buying *your* product
- When the real problem is execution, not strategy
- The question to ask: "Are customers buying in this market? Are they just not buying from us?" If yes → lead bullets.

## Hiring Executives

**Source:** *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*, Chapters 5-6; Y Combinator Lecture 15

### Core Principle: Hire for Strength, Not Lack of Weakness

> "There is no such thing as a great executive. There is only a great executive for a specific company at a specific point in time."

Most hiring processes optimize for eliminating weakness — they screen out risk. Ben argues you should hire for a specific, dominant strength that matches the company's needs right now.

> "Hire for strength, not lack of weakness."

### The Matching Problem

The #1 reason executive hires fail is not incompetence — it's a mismatch between what the executive is great at and what the company needs at this moment.

- A VP of Sales who's great at building enterprise sales orgs from scratch is different from one who's great at scaling an existing one
- A VP of Engineering who's great at shipping v1 is different from one who's great at managing 200 engineers

### Firing Executives

From Bill Campbell:

> "You can take somebody's job. You don't have to take their dignity."

**When talking to the company about a fired executive:**
- Thank them for their work
- Let people know they're moving on
- Don't explain their personal failures publicly
- What you say at that meeting is their reputation — everyone in your company will get a reference call

**When talking to the executive:**
- Be honest: "Here's how we are. Here's what I didn't recognize about us and about you when I made the decision."
- The failure is usually a hiring mistake, not a performance failure
- Start from: "I made a mistake in matching you to our needs"

## The Right Way to Lay People Off

**Source:** *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*, Chapter 6; [a16z.com/the-right-way-to-lay-people-off/](https://a16z.com/the-right-way-to-lay-people-off/); [a16z.com/planning-and-managing-layoffs/](https://a16z.com/planning-and-managing-layoffs/)

Opens with Kanye West, *Touch the Sky*:
> "I'm trying to write my wrongs, but it's funny these same wrongs helped me write this song."

Opsware survived 3 consecutive layoffs (400 employees total), horrible press coverage (WSJ and BusinessWeek cover stories), a stock price of $0.35/share — and still achieved a $1.6B outcome. Doug Leone of Sequoia called it "a complete violation of the laws of venture capital physics."

The reason: they laid people off **the right way**.

### The 6 Steps

**Step 1: Get your head right.**
- You will feel awful. You may feel stupid. ("Ben is either lying or stupid or both." Ben's reaction: "Definitely stupid.")
- Focus on the future, not the past.

**Step 2: Don't delay.**
- Execute as fast as possible after the decision is made.
- If word leaks, managers are put in impossible positions.

**Step 3: Be clear about why.**
- You are NOT "cleaning up performance issues."
- You are laying people off because **the company failed to hit its plan.**
- Own the failure: "The company failed, and in order to move forward, we will have to lose some excellent people."
- A layoff breaks trust. To rebuild trust, you have to come clean.

**Step 4: Train your managers.**
- Golden rule: **Managers must lay off their own people.** Not HR. Not a "more confrontational" peer. Not an outsourcing firm.
- "People won't remember every day they worked for your company, but they will surely remember the day you laid them off."
- Managers should: explain briefly what happened (company failure, not personal); be clear the decision is non-negotiable; be prepared with all benefits details.

**Step 5: Address the entire company.**
- The CEO delivers the message before individual notifications.
- Bill Campbell's insight: **"The message is for the people who are staying."**
- The people who stay will care deeply about how you treat their colleagues.

**Step 6: Be visible, be present.**
- After the announcement, you will want to hide. Don't.
- Be present. Talk to people. Help them carry their things to their car.
- "Do not do this" → go to a bar and drink a fifth of tequila.

### Additional Guidance (from a16z planning doc)

- **Do it once.** Cut deep enough that you don't have to do it again. Multiple rounds destroy trust.
- **It's roles, not people.** Think about what roles to eliminate, not which people to remove.
- **The CEO must own the messaging.** "Employees joined your company because of your vision and you. Now you've failed them. You need to own that."

## Management Debt

**Source:** *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*, Chapter 5; [Big Think video](https://bigthinkmedia.substack.com/p/advice-from-ben-horowitz)

Management debt is the management equivalent of technical debt: expedient short-term decisions that create expensive long-term consequences.

> "Management debt is what happens when you don't do what you're supposed to. I should fire that person, but that's going to be very, very messy, so I'm going to blow it off. I should fill this executive position, but I'm oh so busy with other things, so I'm going to let that go."

### The Compounding Effect

> "And the problem is that debt causes knock-on effects. So you don't just have the problem of the bad executive or bad manager that you didn't fire. Now that debt creates an employee satisfaction problem among everybody on that team. And then the reputation of that team starts to degrade inside the organization. And so now that team can't be effective with other teams. And then you start to have attrition on that team because nobody wants to work in an organization with a bad manager that nobody else in the organization respects. These things cascade, and pretty soon, it's very, very difficult to kind of get out from under it."

### Three Common Forms

1. **Putting two in the box.** Two great employees, one role — splitting the role to keep both creates confusion and lack of accountability.
2. **Overcompensating key employees.** Matching a competing offer to retain one person creates long-term resentment and unsustainable compensation practices.
3. **No performance management system.** Skipping formal feedback and reviews is tempting early, but inevitably leads to dissatisfaction and underperformance.

### The Principle

> "You need to run towards the pain and darkness and not away from it. The best leaders always run towards the darkness. They always run towards a problem. They never run away from it. And the ones that fail always hesitate."

We call it the **kimchi problem**: "The deeper you bury it, the hotter it gets." (Y Combinator Lecture 15)

## Building Culture Deliberately

**Source:** *What You Do Is Who You Are* (2019); [Fortune interview, Oct 2019](https://fortune.com/2019/10/25/ben-horowitz-culture-book)

### Core Thesis

> "Your culture is how your company makes decisions when you're not there. It is how they behave when no one is looking. If you don't methodically set your culture, then two-thirds of it will end up being accidental, and the rest will be a mistake."

> "Who you are is not the values you list on the wall. It's not what you say in company-wide meetings. It's not your marketing campaign. It's not even what you believe. Who you are is what you do."

### The Four Historical Models

Ben draws culture-building lessons from four historical leaders:

1. **Toussaint Louverture** — Led the only successful slave revolt in history (Haiti). Lesson: Culture must serve the mission, and the leader must model it visibly. Applied to: Netflix (Reed Hastings), Uber (Travis Kalanick, as cautionary tale).

2. **The Samurai (Bushido)** — Ruled Japan for 700 years. Lesson: Establish clear, actionable virtues — not values, *virtues*. Values are what you believe; virtues are what you do. Cultural elements are part of a whole; picking and choosing creates contradictions.

3. **Genghis Khan** — Built the world's largest empire. Lesson: Inclusivity as a strategic advantage. Identify unique strengths in each group, absorb them, teach them across the organization. Applied to: Don Thompson (first African-American CEO of McDonald's).

4. **Shaka Senghor** — Convicted of murder, ran the most formidable prison gang, then transformed prison culture. Lesson: Culture can be rebuilt from the inside, even in the most hostile environments.

### Culture-Building Techniques

- **Shocking rules:** Any rule so surprising it makes people ask "Why do we have this rule?" will reinforce key cultural elements.
- **Incorporate outside leadership:** Bring in an experienced person from the culture you aspire to have.
- **Object lessons:** Cement a value with a dramatic, visible act.
- **Visible participation:** "Culture only works if the leader visibly participates in and vocally champions it."

### The Standard Principle

> "There is a saying in the military: if you see something below standard and do nothing, then you have set a new standard."

### Culture as the Strong Force

> "In the short run, product superiority trumps culture. But in the long run, culture can sustain or lead to a superior product org."

## People, Products, Profits

**Source:** *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*, Chapter 5; attributed to Jim Barksdale (CEO of Netscape)

> "We take care of the people, the products, and the profits — in that order."

— Jim Barksdale, as quoted by Ben Horowitz

### What It Means

**Taking care of the people** means your company is a good place to work. Smart people have options — they can work anywhere. If your company is a bad place to work, you will lose the best people first (they have the most options), and you'll be stuck with the people who have nowhere else to go.

**Taking care of the products** means building things that customers actually want and that work well. This follows naturally from taking care of the people — great people, well-supported, build great products.

**Taking care of the profits** is the result, not the driver. If you get people and products right, profits follow. If you start by optimizing for profits and neglect people and products, the whole thing collapses.

### The Order Matters

The ordering is not decorative. It's causal:
- Cut people to boost profits → products degrade → profits collapse
- Neglect product to pursue revenue → customers churn → revenue collapses
- Take care of people → they build great products → products generate profits

## Good Product Manager / Bad Product Manager

**Source:** *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*, Chapter 5; [a16z.com/good-product-manager-bad-product-manager](https://a16z.com/good-product-manager-bad-product-manager)

Originally written as a training document at Netscape. Ben later called it a "stand-alone document that described what I expected" — a way to set standards without micromanaging.

### Key Contrasts

| Good Product Manager | Bad Product Manager |
|---|---|
| CEO of the product; takes full responsibility | Makes excuses |
| Defines the "what" (target), not the "how" | Feels best when figuring out the "how" |
| Thinks in terms of delivering superior value to the market | Gets confused between delivering value, matching features, pricing, ubiquity |
| Communicates crisply in writing and verbally | Communicates poorly, relies on informal direction |
| Decomposes problems | Combines all problems into one |
| Thinks about the story they want the press to write | Tries to cover every feature for the press |
| Creates FAQs, presentations, white papers proactively | Complains about spending all day answering sales questions |
| Takes written positions on important issues | Gives direction informally |
| Gathers information informally | Gives direction informally |
| Asks the press questions | Answers any press question |
| Assumes press and analysts are smart | Assumes press and analysts are dumb |

### How to Use

Ben wrote this as **a training document**, not an evaluation rubric. The purpose is to set clear expectations upfront so people know what "good" looks like. Use it to:
1. Onboard new PMs with explicit standards
2. Self-diagnose: "Am I being a good PM or a bad PM right now?"
3. Set cultural expectations for the product org

## Training and 1-on-1s

**Source:** *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*, Chapter 5; Y Combinator Lecture 15

### On Training

Ben considers training the most underrated management activity:

> "People at McDonald's get trained for their jobs, but people in Silicon Valley startups don't. It makes no sense."

Training is the single highest-leverage activity a manager can do. An hour of training can affect the performance of 10-20 people for months.

### On 1:1s

- The 1:1 is the employee's meeting, not the manager's
- The manager should mostly listen
- The key question is not "What are you working on?" but "What's the biggest obstacle you're facing?"
- 1:1s are where you surface problems early, before they become management debt
- If your direct reports consistently have nothing to say in 1:1s, something is wrong — either they don't trust you or you're not creating space for honesty

### Functional Training Documents

- "Good Product Manager / Bad Product Manager" is an example of a functional training document
- Every functional area should have one: "Good Engineer / Bad Engineer," "Good Designer / Bad Designer"
- These documents set expectations and create a shared definition of excellence

## Ones and Twos

**Source:** *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*, Chapter 7

Ben distinguishes between two types of executives:

### Ones (Visionaries)
- Prefer gathering information and making decisions from first principles
- Hate the process of getting the organization to execute efficiently
- Often the founders, the product visionaries
- Tend to be brilliant but disorganized

### Twos (Operators)
- Love the process of making the organization run efficiently
- Excel at finding the 20 things wrong with a plan and fixing them
- Great at execution, process, scaling
- Tend to be less visionary but more reliable

### The Tension

Every great founding team needs both. The problem comes when a One tries to be a Two (or vice versa):
- A One running operations creates chaos
- A Two running product vision creates mediocrity

> "Every great founding team needs two roles: you need somebody who can build the product... and then you need an entrepreneur, and the entrepreneur's job is to figure out the right product at the right time."

## When to Sell Your Company

**Source:** *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*, Chapter 7

### The Framework

Ben's key questions:

1. **Are you being offered a very large premium?** If someone offers a price far above what you think you're worth, that's worth considering seriously.
2. **Is this an emotional reaction or a rational analysis?** Founders often want to sell when they're exhausted (The Struggle) or want to hold when they're euphoric. Separate the decision from the emotion.
3. **Are you number one or number two in the market?** Being number two means you're potentially one bad quarter from being number three. The acquisition premium may never come again.
4. **What's the probability-weighted outcome of staying independent?** Be honest. Factor in the realistic probability of the $10B outcome, not just the dream.
5. **Can you actually build a standalone company?** Some products are features, not companies. If you're a feature, sell to the platform.

### The Trap

> "If you're going to sell, the best time is when someone wants to buy."

Don't assume you can sell whenever you want. Acquisition interest is perishable. If you pass on a good offer, the next one may not come — or may come at a much lower price.
